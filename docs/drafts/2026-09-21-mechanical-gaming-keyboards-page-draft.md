## Intro (rewritten)

We scored all 204 mechanical gaming keyboards in our database on gaming responsiveness, typing feel, build quality, software, ergonomics and wireless performance, then weighted those scores to rank this list. Prices run from $60 to $400, and every pick's live listing was checked the day this was written. Listings flagged for condition, availability or price are left out of selection automatically.

Two things stand out in the results. Hall Effect boards hold three of the seven slots, from $130 to $250, and they post three of the four best responsiveness scores here. And Keychron still takes three slots on software alone: its picks score 95 to 98, where no other brand on this page gets past 78.

## The three new picks

### AULA — AULA F87 Wireless Mechanical Keyboard — $60 — budget

**Hot-swap, PBT keycaps and tri-mode for $60**

The F87 is the cheapest pick here at $60, and the spec sheet reads like a board that costs more: hot-swappable sockets, PBT keycaps, and tri-mode connection over 2.4GHz, Bluetooth 5.0 or USB-C. Typing feel scores 75 on pre-lubed switches, with a dampened sound that's pleasant for its class.

It's the one to buy if you want to find out whether you like mechanical keyboards without spending $140 to do it. The compact layout keeps the keys most people need. Gaming responsiveness is 70, the lowest on this page: reliable for most games, and twenty-five points behind the fastest boards here.

Where the $60 shows: software scores 55, by far the lowest on the list. It handles basic RGB and key remapping and not much else. Build is 65, mostly plastic, sturdy enough for daily use but nothing an enthusiast would admire. If remapping matters to you, the 40-point software gap to the K8 HE is what the extra $80 buys.

### Glorious — Glorious GMMK 3 PRO HE — $250 — preset:streamer

**Aluminum Hall Effect board with clunky software**

Streamer weighting puts typing feel first (90), then software (85) and responsiveness (75). Of the boards not already holding a slot above, the GMMK 3 PRO HE comes out on top: 95 gaming responsiveness from Hall Effect magnetic switches with rapid trigger and 8K polling, 92 build from a CNC-machined aluminum chassis, and 85 typing feel on a gasket mount. The 75% layout with a rotary knob scores 82 on ergonomics.

It also takes both magnetic and standard MX switches in the same sockets, which its own data calls unmatched for hardware customization.

Now the costs, and there are three. Software scores 78 because the Glorious Core app is clunky and buggy, a real problem on a board whose best tricks are set in software. Wireless is 75, the joint-lowest of the wireless picks, and the battery drains fast with 8K polling and bright RGB. The tall front edge practically requires a wrist rest. At $250 it's $110 more than the K8 HE, which gives up five points of speed and gains seventeen on software.

### NuPhy — Air75 HE Rapid Trigger Wired Custom Magnetic Gaming — $130 — overall

**Low-profile Hall Effect, best ergonomics, $130**

The NuPhy Air75 HE rounds out the list as the cheapest way into Hall Effect here: 92 gaming responsiveness from Magnetic Jade switches with rapid trigger, at $130. That's $10 under the K8 HE and two points quicker.

What sets it apart is the shape. It's a low-profile 75% board, and it scores 90 on ergonomics, the best on this page, because you can type on it comfortably without a wrist rest. Build is 88 with an aluminum top frame and PBT keycaps, and typing feel is 85 on pre-lubed switches.

Two plain drawbacks. This model is strictly wired, so there's no wireless score at all, and every other pick on the page has one. And software scores 78: the web-based configurator works for setting actuation points but lacks polish. If you want wireless and deeper remapping, that's the $10 argument for the K8 HE instead.

## FAQs (questions unchanged; answers 1–3 rewritten, 4–5 adjusted)

**Are Hall Effect switches worth it over regular mechanical ones?**

For gaming, they're the most meaningful switch upgrade on this list. Hall Effect switches read how far you press rather than just whether you pressed, which lets you set the actuation point and register faster. Three picks here have them: the NuPhy Air75 HE scores 92 on gaming responsiveness at $130, the K8 HE 90 at $140, and the Glorious GMMK 3 PRO HE 95 at $250. For typing alone, you won't notice the difference.

**Why do keyboard prices swing so much month to month?**

Because this category discounts constantly and Amazon pricing is volatile. Since we last recorded prices, the Azoth Extreme fell from $440 to $400 and the Q6 Max rose from $200 to $220, while the K8 HE and the Q2 Max didn't move at all. The prices here reflect the listings on the day we checked. If a board you want sits at the top of your budget, watching it for a few weeks is a genuinely effective strategy.

**What does QMK/VIA support actually get me?**

Open firmware — you remap keys, build layers, and record macros in a browser instead of a vendor app, and the settings live on the board itself. It's why the Keychron picks score 95 to 98 on software while nothing else on this page gets past 78. If you plug into multiple machines or hate background utilities, that gap is the clearest dividing line on this list.

**Is a heavy aluminum keyboard actually better to type on?**

It's better to listen to. Mass absorbs the ping and hollowness that plastic cases resonate with, which is why our two all-aluminum Keychrons score 90 and 95 on typing feel. What it doesn't buy is speed — the Q2 Max scores 85 on responsiveness against the $140 K8 HE's 90. Weight also makes a board a desk fixture rather than something you move around.

**How small a keyboard should I go?**

Depends on what you'll miss. The 65% Q2 Max scores 75 on ergonomics because you lose the function row and arrows to key combinations. The low-profile 75% NuPhy Air75 HE goes the other way and scores 90 on ergonomics, the best here. If you use arrows daily or type numbers, the full-size Q6 Max or the TKL K8 HE will annoy you far less than reclaiming four inches of desk will please you.
